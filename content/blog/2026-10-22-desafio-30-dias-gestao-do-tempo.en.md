Yesterday I attended an internal talk by [Bruno Matos](https://www.linkedin.com/in/brunommatos/) (Group Product Manager), at the company, called "Time is attention - time management in the age of AI". I really enjoy the topic of organization and time management, and at the end Bruno suggested a 30-day challenge applying the tips he shared. I decided to take it on, and this post is the starting point!

## The provocation

The opening uses a calculation from Oliver Burkeman's book *Four Thousand Weeks*: an 80-year life has a little over 4,000 weeks. The idea isn't to cause panic, but to change the question. Since demands are infinite and time isn't, every calendar is a set of trade-offs. The difference lies in making those trade-offs deliberately or letting them happen by accident.

Then came the question: **of the last 4 weeks, how many did you spend mostly on what really matters?** My mental answer was: I don't know, because I don't measure it.

## The real problem: not a lack of hours, but fragmentation

The diagnosis part was the one that hit me hardest as a **tech lead**. A few points:

- **The day got chopped up.** Recent Microsoft data shows people being interrupted every few minutes during core hours, with a good share of meetings landing right in the windows of peak focus.
- **Every context switch charges a toll.** Researcher Gloria Mark shows it takes quite a while to get back to a task after an interruption, and Sophie Leroy calls *attention residue* the part of your mind that stays stuck on the previous task. The counterintuitive detail is that **we interrupt ourselves almost as often as others interrupt us**.
- **Maker vs. manager.** In Paul Graham's essay, makers work in half-days and managers work in 30-minute slots. A 2 p.m. meeting doesn't cost a programmer one hour: it breaks the whole afternoon. As a tech lead, I live on both schedules at once, and that's **my main challenge**.

## Some mental models I'll take with me

- **The urgent hijacks the important.** The Eisenhower matrix is an old friend, but there's research showing we prefer the task with a short deadline even when it's worth less. The deadline fools the brain and feels like importance. That's why study, **architecture and people development, which nobody demands by a date, always lose ground**.
- **We're optimists by design.** We underestimate deadlines (the planning fallacy) and, when there's time left, work expands to fill it all (Parkinson's Law). The suggested antidotes: look at the history of similar tasks instead of the plan, and decide how much time something *deserves* instead of how long it will *take*.
- **Fewer things in parallel.** By Little's Law, with the same throughput, fewer open fronts mean faster deliveries. This applies to squads and to people.
- **For those who lead, time is leverage.** Inspired by Andy Grove: answering pings all day affects one person for a few hours; a good decision document or a development 1:1 affects many people for a long time.

## And where does AI fit in?

The point I liked most: **AI doesn't create time, it changes the cost of each task**. The rule of thumb is to look at two axes: how much it costs to produce and how much it costs to verify.

**If reviewing the result costs almost as much as doing it**, AI saves nothing, it just shifts the effort. The sweet spot is work that's expensive to produce and cheap to check.

One of the examples was precisely a calendar audit: connect your calendar to an assistant and ask it to classify events by category, add up the hours and point out how many long meeting-free blocks were left. Doing it by hand takes hours; checking takes minutes, because you recognize your own meetings.

## The 30-day challenge

The talk ends with a four-step challenge:

1. **Measure:** audit your calendar and write a time budget (how much you want to dedicate to each type of activity).
2. **Protect:** reserve two 90-minute blocks per week, each with a concrete intention in the format "when X, I do Y".
3. **Automate:** turn a recurring task into an AI routine.
4. **Review:** compare the plan with reality.

## How I'll do it

I'm making one adaptation: measuring will last two weeks, and protecting starts alongside it, in the second week.

The reason is that I already have the habit of logging work as focus blocks on my calendar. But, looking closely, I realized most of those blocks are created after the work has happened. That's logging, not protecting. Protecting, in the sense of the challenge, means deciding on the block beforehand (ideally the day before), with a start, an end, a deliverable and an intention like "when X, I do Y".

So the first week serves as a baseline: I log everything as I always have, without changing anything. In the second, I keep measuring, but start protecting blocks in advance. That way I can compare one week with the other and see the effect of protecting in the numbers. In practice, that's four weeks (28 days), one for each step:

| Step | Period |
|---|---|
| Measure (baseline) | Oct 22 to Oct 28 |
| Measure + protect | Oct 29 to Nov 4 |
| Automate | Nov 5 to Nov 11 |
| Review | Nov 12 to Nov 18 |

To measure, I created categories in my Google Calendar and each event now gets a color:

- 🔁 Team rituals (blueberry)
- 👀 Code review (light gray)
- 📚 Study (pink)
- 🚨 Support (red)
- 🔨 Development (yellow)
- 🧭 Leadership and people (green)
- ✅ QA and validation (purple)
- 🌱 Personal (dark gray)

One rule I'll follow: the color shows the type of activity, and the event title still shows the project. That way I can answer two different questions: how much time goes to code versus meetings, and how much goes to each front.

At the end of the two weeks, I want to answer with numbers:

- How many hours went to each category?
- How many meeting-free 90-minute blocks did I have per week?
- How much of my coding time was planned and how much was reactive?

I won't define the ideal budget yet. I first want to see the real picture, without bias, and only then decide where I want the hours to go.
